14.1 · Solar Power

Three Ways to Capture the Sun

The National Renewable Energy Laboratory estimates that 14 million acres of solar panels, roughly the size of Lake Michigan, could power the entire United States.

Advantages

  • Freely available and nondepletable
  • No combustion emissions
  • Rapidly deployable on rooftops, parking lots and fields
  • Rooftop collectors also heat water stored in tanks, cutting water-heating costs

Challenges

  • Needs consistent sunlight: output varies with weather and time of day, so storage is needed
  • Mining the minerals in panels carries its own environmental trade-offs

Lake Michigan outline: Natural Earth 1:50m (about 58,000 km²), drawn to the same scale as a 14-million-acre square (56,700 km²).

14.2 · Hydropower

Water Power: 6% of Global Energy

⟶⟶⟶⟶⟶
0 MW

Power = water’s weight × drop × flow × efficiency

How it works

Flowing water spins turbines. Dams store water; run-of-river systems use the natural flow without large reservoirs.

Advantages

A constant supply (not weather-dependent), no combustion emissions, and a long operating life.

Trade-offs

1Fish migration blocked 2sediment trapped 3flooded vegetation releases methane 4communities displaced: Three Gorges Dam displaced over 1 million people.

Illustrative dam: head 60–180 m, flow up to 800 m³/s, 90% efficiency. Homes assume about 10,500 kWh a year per U.S. home (EIA).

14.5 · Transition Drivers

What Could Drive a Transition Away from Fossil Fuels?

I

Falling Costs

The cost of wind and solar has fallen steadily for decades. Solar jobs now greatly outnumber coal jobs and economics are decisively shifting.

II

Government Policy

R&D funding, tax incentives, and utility purchase mandates have driven wind and solar growth in Texas, Iowa, California, and Arizona.

III

The Solutions Project

Stanford/UC Berkeley researchers mapped how all 50 US states could transition to 100% renewable energy by 2050 using available technology.

IV

Grid & Storage Innovation

Smart grids, microgrids, and V2G technology improve resilience and allow variable wind and solar to reliably meet demand.

Chapter 14 · Key Concepts

Energy Alternatives: How Are Our Energy Decisions Changing?

Photovoltaic (PV) Panel
Silicon panel producing electricity when exposed to sunlight
Variable Generation
Power output that fluctuates with weather or time of day
Hydropower
Water spinning turbines; 6% of global energy; ecosystem trade-offs
Nuclear Fission
Splitting uranium nuclei to generate heat and electricity
Biofuel / Ethanol
Transportation fuel from fermented plant sugars blended with gasoline
Electric Vehicle (EV)
4× more efficient than gas; zero direct emissions
Cogeneration
Waste heat from power plants reused for heating or cooling
Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G)
EV batteries supply stored power back to the electrical grid
