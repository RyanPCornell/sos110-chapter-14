What is the purpose of this calculator?

This is an advanced charging calculator that allows the user to factor in a variety of variables to determine the feasibility of different charging scenarios. It is aimed at policymakers, researchers, and other expert users.

What will the model tell us?

The model simulates hundreds of months of driving and calculates how likely it is that home-charging would sufficiently charge your car over a month of driving. A probability of 99% indicates that there is a 99% chance you would not need to visit a Level 3 fast-charger during a normal month.

A probability of 10% indicates the driver would likely need to stop at a high-speed charger one or more times during the month. The model also estimates the average number of Level 3 stops required per month across all simulated months.

Configure your vehicle, charger type, driving habits, and conditions in the panel on the left, then click Run Simulation. Use at least 500 iterations for stable results — at lower counts (e.g. 200), probability estimates can differ by 10–14 percentage points between runs purely from random sampling, even when both runs are statistically correct.

How does the model work?

The model simulates hundreds of months of driving. Each month's daily mileage is drawn from a negative binomial distribution parameterized by your average mileage and the variability setting — calibrated on national driving survey data. The shape and spread of each simulated month reflects realistic day-to-day variation.

Each simulated day, the car's range decreases by the day's miles. If the battery falls to the fast-charge threshold, a Level 3 stop is tallied and the battery resets to maximum range. At the end of each day, home charging is added (capped at maximum range). The simulation repeats for all 31 days of a month.

The probability output is the share of simulated months where the car never hit the fast-charge threshold. The stops output is the mean number of Level 3 stops across all simulated months. Charts show these values across the full range of daily-miles scenarios (10–70 mi/day).

How is the Level 1 charge rate calculated?

The model assumes a standard outlet at 110V / 12A = 1.32 kW/hr. Per-mile efficiency values are from EPA data, which already accounts for charging losses.

Did you know EVs can charge from a standard outlet?

This type of charging (Level 1) is slow, but incredibly practical — drivers charge while they sleep or while the car sits at home. No special equipment needed beyond a standard 110V wall outlet.

What will the model tell us?

The model calculates how likely it is that a standard outlet would fully cover your monthly charging needs. A probability of 99% means there is a 99% chance you would not need to visit a fast-charger during a normal month.

A probability of 10% means Level 1 charging alone would likely be insufficient and the driver would need at least one fast-charger stop per month. The second output estimates the average number of those stops.

How does the model work?

The model simulates hundreds of months of driving. Each month's daily mileage is drawn from a negative binomial distribution calibrated on national driving survey data, capturing realistic day-to-day variation.

Each simulated day the car's range decreases by the day's miles. If the battery falls to 40 miles remaining, a Level 3 stop is tallied and the battery resets to full range. Home charging (Level 1) is then added at the end of each day. The probability output is the share of months with no fast-charger stop needed.

What is "Model Speed"?

Standard runs 400 simulated months — fast and suitable for most comparisons. Higher Precision runs 1,000 months, reducing run-to-run variation but taking significantly longer.

How is the Level 1 charge rate calculated?

The model assumes a standard outlet at 110V / 12A = 1.32 kW/hr. EPA efficiency data is used directly, so charging losses are already accounted for.
